No, cold email is not dead. What died is sending at volume. In 6 micro campaigns I ran over the last two and a half months, 50 to 100 people each, the average reply rate was 40%, and about half the replies were positive. No new tool did that. A small list, real research on every company, several contacts and channels, and an email that survives the first second did.

## Why does everyone say cold email is dead?

In classic B2B, email was for years a channel you could run at a very, very large scale. Hundreds and thousands of emails, cheap, because all you paid for was mailboxes and infrastructure. And it worked: it built pipeline.

Over the last five or six years, more and more people say the channel is dead. For two reasons.

One, spam filters got much better. Most of those emails never reach the inbox. They land in spam or some other folder. Since February 2024, [Gmail requires bulk senders](https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126) to authenticate their domain and keep reported spam below 0.3%. Sending thousands of cold emails a day makes that hard.

Two, the AI flood. Everyone produces content, and it all looks the same. Recipients feel it: in [Hunter's 2026 report](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email), 69% of US decision makers said it bothers them when an email was written by AI.

The numbers follow. A classic email campaign always got 1% to 5% replies. Replies, not positive replies. Today it is even less: [a Belkins study of 7.5 million emails](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates) found an average reply rate of 0.45% across 2025 campaigns. Hunter's data on 31 million emails sent in 2025 puts the average at 4.5%. [An analysis of 2 million emails from February 2026](https://www.apollo.io/insights/what-is-a-good-benchmark-for-reply-rates-in-cold-outreach) found 2.09% replies, and only 14.1% of them positive. You need hundreds or thousands of emails to move the needle on pipeline.

For a few years, I also thought the channel was dead.

## What is a micro campaign?

A micro campaign is a small cold outreach campaign, 50 to 100 people, where every company is researched before contact and approached from several directions: several relevant contacts, and several channels in a sensible order. Email, LinkedIn, phone.

It started 3 months ago. I was working for clients who wanted partnerships: distributors, business partners, and small startups looking for their first customers and design partners.

I call companies like these, with no offense, "nobody". Early on, a company has no name in the market, so nobody is searching for it. Its natural way in is cold outreach: on LinkedIn, by phone, or by email. ([We also wrote about what founders carry at this stage](/en/solutions/founders).)

## What reply rate can a micro campaign get?

The first two campaigns amazed me. 40% replies. And close to half of those were positive.

I was sure it was a mistake. Maybe this audience was not busy, maybe they reply to anything. So I ran 4 more. 6 campaigns in total.

The numbers held:

| | Classic email campaign | Micro campaign (6 campaigns) |
|---|---|---|
| Size | hundreds to thousands | 50 to 100 people |
| Reply rate | 2.09% | 30% to 50%, average 40% |
| Positive replies (of all replies) | 14.1% | about 50% |
| Outreach needed for one positive reply | about 340 emails | about 5 people |

**A micro campaign is 68 times more efficient than a classic email campaign.**

The math behind the last row: in a classic campaign, 2.09% replies times 14.1% positive gives one positive reply per about 340 emails. In a micro campaign, 40% times 50% gives one positive reply for about every 5 people.

Positive means: the message interests me, let's talk. From there, it depends on your offer and your sales execution (and that is a topic for another post...).

I did not rush to conclusions. Not after one campaign, not after two. 6 campaigns is still not a study. But the direction matches the large datasets: in Hunter's data, campaigns of up to 50 recipients got 6.2% replies, against 2.4% for campaigns over 500. Small lists win, even where the gap is less dramatic.

## Why do micro campaigns work when bulk email does not?

I look at an email as three levels. You have to pass all of them.

### Does the email reach the inbox at all?

That is a question of who sends it, and from which mailbox. The classic method said: send from a cold mailbox, warm it up, make it look real. I think the filters do a good job of catching that.

On the other hand, you cannot send thousands of emails from the CEO's mailbox. It would burn it. In a micro campaign that does not happen, because the volume is small.

### Does the first second create interest?

At first glance, subject and first line, I decide whether this might be relevant to me. If I think this person really knows something about me, and it does not look like a generic email, or worse, a generic AI email, I give it a few more seconds.

### Is the content itself relevant?

I split the body into three parts:

1. Show me you know something about me.
2. Tell me about you, and why you are relevant to me.
3. Lead me to one reasonable ask, something I would be willing to give you.

Hit all three, and you have a real chance of a reply.

## How do you build a micro campaign, step by step?

1. **A small, focused list.** 50 to 100 people, one audience you can speak to in one language.
2. **Research on every company.** Make no mistake, I did not research each company by hand. I used AI tools. But I did not give up on quality or targeting (I did not let the AI get lazy on me... it has that tendency).
3. **Several contacts per company.** Not only the decision maker, also the people who influence them.
4. **Several channels, in a sensible order.** Email, LinkedIn and phone, so each touch continues the one before.
5. **A real mailbox.** The CEO's, or whoever owns the relationship, at a volume it can carry.
6. **The three parts in every email.** What I know about you, why I am relevant, one reasonable ask.

At Ankora, an AI-based operations management company, we run campaigns like these for clients. AI does the research and the drafts, and a person picks the list and reviews before anything goes out. ([This is how it works](/en/how-it-works).)

## What should you do now?

1. Take your next campaign and cut it to 50 to 100 people.
2. For each recipient, write one line that proves you know something specific about them.
3. Check the ask: would a stranger agree to it? A 15-minute call, not an hour-long demo.
4. Send from a real mailbox, a small number per day.
5. Measure positive replies, not opens.

Simple math: at 4% replies, I would have to send huge volumes to get the same meetings. At 40%, with half the replies positive, I do not need to send thousands.

Exactly when everything is flooded with content, what used to work works again, if you sacrifice volume and not quality.
